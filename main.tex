\section{Abstract}

\textbf{Rationale} Across the globe, drug regulation is being reconsidered. This includes traditionally legal and illegal substances. The Netherlands is no exception. Examples include a national experiment regulating cannabis, tobacco sales being limited to specialty stores and a state commission favourable towards MDMA assisted therapy. These changes are mirrored by changes in public attitudes towards drug regulation.  \newline
\textbf{State of literature} Most notably, global support for the regulation of cannabis has risen markedly, while support for the commercial regulation of tobacco (and alcohol?) has declined. In the Netherlands too, public support for fully regulating cannabis has risen to approximately 80\%. For most illicit drugs however the picture is less clear. One good source is the 2014 youth Eurobarometer. It found that across EU member states support for regulating mdma hovered between 1\% and 15\% of Europeans aged 18 to 32. \newline
\textbf{Literature gap} However, the existing literature comes with notable limitations. First, existing measures frame support for drug regulation in binary or ternary terms: prohibition, regulation or unrestricted access. Second, existing samples are not representative of adult national populations. Third, scientific samples are all over a decade old. Fourth, there is limited work modelling attitudes towards drug regulation, in part because it's usually elicited in binary or ternary terms. \newline
\textbf{Our contribution} The present study asks Dutch adults (n=1239) to choose between nine forms of regulated supply for nine different drugs, in decreasing order of restrictiveness: full prohibition, research only, treatment only, on prescription only, sale at speciality store without or with profit, licensed sale by third parties without or with advertisements, and unrestricted access. Further variables collected and an exploratory network analysis modelling support for drug regulation were based on the Causal Attitude Network (CAN) model.  \newline

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Shifting regulation worldwide and in the Netherlands} Regulatory models which were unthinkable decades ago are now in place: commercial legalization of cannabis in many US states and a ban on tobacco sales to anybody born after 2008 in the UK \parencite{tobaccovapesact2026}.

\textbf{Shifting attitudes towards regulation worldwide and in the Netherlands} Attitudes towards the regulation of psychoactive substances seem to be changing in the West \parencite{felson2019,gallup2023}. 

\textbf{No proper attitude research trying to understsand this shift} As described above, the public is usually consulted in impoverished terms. And government communication is sometimes counterproductive \parencite[e.g., messaging that presents drug use as normalised may increase use,][]{vanwingerde2023}. How we discuss drug regulation matters: in an Irish citizens' assembly 85\% rejected the status quo, 58.6\% chose a hybrid of different approaches for different drugs, and no universal option drew more than 18.4\% \parencite{citizensassembly2024}.

To date no instrument measures the full structure of beliefs, feelings and behaviour underlying drug regulation preferences \parencite[but see][]{greer2019,wilkins2022,hamrak2026}. Similarly, to date no instrument has been developed that measures drug policy preferences as an ordered set of regulatory models per substance. Combining the two yields support for nine regulatory models across nine substances together with the attitude dynamics behind them, usable for instance in public health campaigns.

\textbf{The scale} Drug regulation is inherently multidimensional. For example, more prohibitive policies go hand in hand with more punitive measures (cite?). Nonetheless, we've tried to formulate a unidimensional, and, crucially, a strictly ordinal, scale. Following table ... in \citet{maccoun2001}, we define the main dimension of drug regulation as the policy's 'restrictiveness'. This led to the nine options shown in figure \ref{fig:policy_scale}.

\subsection{Research questions}

\textbf{RQ1.} Where does the Dutch public stand on the regulation spectrum, per substance, and what do those positions rest on? Respondents place nine substances on a nine-point scale of described regulatory models (Figure~\ref{fig:policy_scale}). We describe the distribution per substance and explain it using the attitude items. The result is a map of the debate: which regulatory models are supported, and which beliefs, feelings and behaviours mark out the competing positions.

Three elements of a cleavage: social structure, group identifications and shared moral outlooks, and their reinforcement by parties’ differing ideological supply (our elaboration, based on Bartolini and Mair 1990; Lipset and Rokkan 1967; Bornschier 2010). Broćić and Miles 2021). [...] Zollinger reconstructs that far right voters’ self-descriptions typically center on ‘the dignity of a simple, honest life built around selfreliance on hard work and common sense, whereas new left voters highlight their self-perceived openness, tolerance, dynamism, and unorthodox lifestyle’ (ibid., 19)" 

Importantly, most lay people do not reason in all-encompassing ideological terms. Rather, we consider this divide as providing heuristics for laypeople to reason about complex topics. 


\textbf{interpretation policy preference}

Previous measurements of preference for drug regulation have found far higher support for banning of all illicit drugs in our sample. It thus seems that by parsing drug regulation into a spectrum from total ban to total free market, leads to more people considering and subsequently supporting some form of regulation.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

\textbf{limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The sample was an opt-in sample from the Kieskompas panel. However, invitations were sent out based on stratification. And the sample was weighted. Nonetheless, the findings may therefore in part reflect sample characteristics. It seems especially likely that the current sample is overrepresented by people with an interest in politics. This can in part be seen in the involvement question: do drugs weight in on your voting behaviour, which a majority gave a response on the top half of the scale. Thus, while our findings clearly suggest that our new measurement leads to more people supporting some form of regulation, replications, preferably using probability-samples, are needed to confirm this. 
    \item For the illicit drugs we see support for banning between 34 and 64 percent.
    \item The attitudes items don't explain currently legal drugs well. In part perhaps because we are missing arguments in favour of commercialisation, beyond competing with the illicit market. In other words, why don't people want to go to government stores for alcohol? Out of laziness? Distrust of the government? 
\end{enumerate}


\textbf{future directions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item This is a null meting. Future work may explore experimental applications. For example, there is a status-quo bias. People might not be familiar with the status quo. It would interesting to see the effect of simply sharing the status quo with people. 
\end{enumerate}
