\section{Abstract}

\textbf{Rationale} Across the globe, drug regulation is being reconsidered. This includes traditionally legal and illegal substances. The Netherlands is no exception. Examples include a national experiment regulating cannabis, tobacco sales being limited to specialty stores and a state commission favourable towards MDMA assisted therapy. These changes are mirrored by changes in public attitudes towards drug regulation.  \newline
\textbf{State of literature} Most notably, global support for the regulation of cannabis has risen markedly, while support for the commercial regulation of tobacco (and alcohol?) has declined. In the Netherlands too, public support for fully regulating cannabis has risen to approximately 80\%. For most illicit drugs however the picture is less clear. One good source is the 2014 youth Eurobarometer. It found that across EU member states support for regulating mdma hovered between 1\% and 15\% of Europeans aged 18 to 32. \newline
\textbf{Literature gap} However, the existing literature comes with notable limitations. First, existing measures frame support for drug regulation in binary or ternary terms: prohibition, regulation or unrestricted access. Second, existing samples are not representative of adult national populations. Third, scientific samples are all over a decade old. Fourth, there is limited work modelling attitudes towards drug regulation, in part because it's usually elicited in binary or ternary terms. \newline
\textbf{Our contribution} The present study asks Dutch adults (n=1000) to choose between nine forms of regulated supply for eight different drugs, in decreasing order of restrictiveness: full prohibition, research only, treatment only, on receipt only, sale at speciality store without or with profit, licensed sale by third parties without or with advertisements, and unrestricted access. Further variables collected and an exploratory network analysis modelling support for drug regulation were based on the Causal Attitude Network (CAN) model.  \newline

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Shifting regulation worldwide and in the Netherlands} Regulatory models which were unthinkable decades ago are now in place: commercial legalization of cannabis in many US states and a ban on tobacco sales to anybody born after 2008 in the UK \parencite{tobaccovapesact2026}.

\textbf{Shifting attitudes towards regulation worldwide and in the Netherlands} Attitudes towards the regulation of psychoactive substances seem to be changing in the West \parencite{felson2019,gallup2023}. 

\textbf{No proper attitude research trying to understsand this shift} As described above, the public is usually consulted in impoverished terms. And government communication is sometimes counterproductive \parencite[e.g., messaging that presents drug use as normalised may increase use,][]{vanwingerde2023}. How we discuss drug regulation matters: in an Irish citizens' assembly 85\% rejected the status quo, 58.6\% chose a hybrid of different approaches for different drugs, and no universal option drew more than 18.4\% \parencite{citizensassembly2024}.

To date no instrument measures the full structure of beliefs, feelings and behaviour underlying drug regulation preferences \parencite[but see][]{greer2019,wilkins2022,hamrak2026}. Similarly, to date no instrument has been developed that measures drug policy preferences as an ordered set of regulatory models per substance. Combining the two yields support for seven regulatory models across nine substances together with the attitude dynamics behind them, usable for instance in public health campaigns.

\textbf{The scale} Drug regulation is inherently multidimensional. For example, more prohibitive policies go hand in hand with more punitive measures (cite?). Nonetheless, we've tried to formulate a unidimensional, and, crucially, a strictly ordinal, scale. Following table ... in \citet{maccoun2001}, we define the main dimension of drug regulation as the policy's 'restrictiveness'. This led to the nine options shown in figure \ref{fig:policy_scale}.

\section{Methods}

\textbf{Sample} Two pilots studies were conducted: a convenience sample ($n = 121$) and quota sample of Dutch adults ($n = 120$). Item screening followed the reporting standards discussed in \parencite{burger2022,chambon2026}: item redundancy based on $\rho$, leave-one-out $\Delta R^2$, corrected item-rest. The main study consisted of a single cross-sectional wave taken through Kieskompas (n=1239). The resulting sample was weighted by Kieskompas using iterative post-stratification. Weights were based on the marginal distributions, taken from the Central Bureau of Statistics, of: age, gender, education, migration-background, Nielsen area's, and elicited voting behaviour in last parliamentary elections. 

\textbf{Questionnaire} Wording is at B1 Dutch level. Own-use items are anonymous and skippable. The survey closes with a referral to the Drugs Infolijn.



\textbf{Analysis.} RQ1 is descriptive and exploratory. Per substance the location, dispersion and modality will be reported. The policy sliders are regressed on the attitude items. RQ2 uses a mixed graphical model, estimated with EBIC model selection \parencite{haslbeck2020}, with bootstrapped edge accuracy and centrality stability \parencite{epskamp2018}. Involvement enters as continuous moderator of edge weights \parencite{haslbeck2021}. Modality is estimated per involvement quartile \parencite{hoffstadt2026}.

\subsection{Research questions}.

\textbf{RQ1.} Where does the Dutch public stand on the regulation spectrum, per substance, and what do those positions rest on? Respondents place nine substances on a 0--100 slider anchored by described policy models (see Section~\ref{sec:content} for substances and anchors). We describe the distribution per substance and explain it using the attitude items. The result is a map of the debate: which regulatory models are supported, and which beliefs, feelings and behaviours mark out the competing positions.

\textbf{RQ2.} How does the structure of a respondent's attitude network relate to their position on the drug regulation spectrum? The ternary model predicts that cautious, involved respondents hold stable middle positions. Under the binary model an involved respondent sits at one of the extremes. We are interested in under which conditions an involved middle appears.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

\textbf{limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The attitudes items don't explain currently legal drugs well. In part perhaps because we are missing arguments in favour of commercialisation, beyond competing with the illicit market. In other words, why don't people want to go to government stores for alcohol? Out of laziness? Distrust of the government? 
\end{enumerate}


\textbf{future directions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item This is a null meting. Future work may explore experimental applications. For example, there is a status-quo bias. People might not be familiar with the status quo. It would interesting to see the effect of simply sharing the status quo with people. 
\end{enumerate}
